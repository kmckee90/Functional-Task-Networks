    Fully Autonomous Continual Learning and Knowledge Transfer
               via Cortex-Inspired Neural Assemblies
                                            Kevin L. McKee

                                            Astera Institute



                                                 Abstract
         Continual learning requires both parameter isolation to protect task-specific computation,
     and parameter reuse to transfer useful computation to later tasks. We introduce Functional Task
     Networks (FTNs), a unified mechanism of continual learning and knowledge transfer modeled after
     several properties of the mammalian neocortex. An FTN is formulated as a large, distributed,
     stochastic mixture of experts in which a sparse routing mask is computed at inference time over
     a small support sample of input-output pairs. Per-batch reconfiguration during training, together
     with mask-overlap-filtered replay, shapes a basis of overlapping, partially trained subnetworks.
     Unrelated tasks route through mostly separate assemblies, related tasks overlap on shared
     components. On block-sequentially trained continual learning and knowledge transfer tasks
     including shuffled MNIST for concept shift, Permuted MNIST for domain shift, and Rotated
     MNIST for knowledge transfer, FTN retains solutions nearly as well as disjoint-mask isolation
     and attains the lowest pre-update head-start loss among matched-architecture comparators. The
     learned mask-overlap geometry tracks the known concept-sharing structure where available. We
     show experimentally that under correlated concept shift, support-set mask reconfiguration is
     necessary even for retention. Under domain shift, the distributed bank with replay is sufficient to
     handle retention without the configurer. Forward transfer, or head-start, and backward transfer,
     are also contributed by support-set mask configuration. We further implement FTN-LoRA,
     a bank of Low Rank Adaptation modules on a frozen Qwen2.5-1.5B backbone, and test over
     a six-task multilingual classification stream, demonstrating minimal forgetting and positive
     backward transfer in a scaled setting.


1    Introduction
For continual learning in neural networks, shared parameters let a new task reuse representations
learned from earlier tasks, but the same shared parameters can be overwritten by later gradients,
producing catastrophic forgetting [McCloskey and Cohen, 1989, French, 1999]. Parameter isolation
methods protect earlier computations by assigning tasks to separate subnetworks [Mallya and
Lazebnik, 2018, Serra et al., 2018, Wortsman et al., 2020], but isolation weakens forward transfer
because new tasks cannot easily recruit useful structure from old ones. A central question for
general continual learning solutions is therefore how a model might automatically modulate between
isolating and sharing its parameters.

Concept and domain shift. Continual-learning benchmarks differ in what changes between
tasks. Under domain shift the input distribution changes while the labeling rule remains broadly
compatible. The most generic way to prevent forgetting under domain shift is shuffled replay-based
methods [Lopez-Paz and Ranzato, 2017, Chaudhry et al., 2019, Buzzega et al., 2020], in which data

                                                     1
from previous tasks are mixed in training minibatches. The gradients over the tasks are averaged,
so no single task dominates all of the weight updates. Under concept shift the labeling rule or
output function itself changes, so the same input must be mapped to different targets in different
tasks. Methods like Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) and Synaptic Intelligence [Kirkpatrick
et al., 2017, Zenke et al., 2017] address domain shift but not concept shift. Penalizing drift on
parameters that need to compute different functions on different tasks only blocks learning. Naive
replay similarly fails because two replayed labels for the same input contradict each other unless
replay is routed through different subnetworks.

Block-sequential training. Block sequential training presents a maximally difficult regime for
retention than i.i.d. minibatching: each long stretch of updates is dominated by one task at a time,
intensifying forgetting on other tasks. It is also the schedule of most realistic continual deployments
(sequential adapter installation, on-the-job fine-tuning, sliding-window streams). We specifically
target this training regime as a necessary part of the continual learning problem definition.

Functional Task Networks. We introduce Functional Task Networks (FTNs), a support-set-
conditioned routing mechanism for continual learning without task identity. FTN uses a large
bank of small but deep networks to learn modular but distributed task representations. During
both training and inference, FTN selects a sparse assembly for each minibatch by optimizing a
binary top-k mask to minimize loss with respect to a small labeled support set. This gives a single
mechanism for both parameter isolation and reuse: unrelated tasks can be routed through mostly
different assemblies, while related tasks can overlap on components that carry useful computation.
In this paper, we evaluate whether this mechanism produces the intended trade-off: retention under
block-sequential training and concept shift, forward transfer before any update on a new task, and
mask-overlap structure that reflects task relatedness when such structure is known. We show that
FTN occupies a middle regime between full parameter sharing and fixed disjoint masks, retaining
most of the protection of isolation while enabling recombination that fixed task masks cannot
provide.

Biological motivation. FTN is motivated by several features of neocortical computation that
suggest a route to sparse, reusable, and partially overlapping task representations. Sparsification
via top-k masking has previously served as an abstraction for the effects of inhibitory competition
among neurons[Yang et al., 2019, Cichon and Gan, 2015, Mongillo et al., 2018, Rolls, 2010, Khona
and Fiete, 2022, Amari, 1977, Maass, 2000, Douglas and Martin, 2004]. Our choice to use a large
number of deep networks with low-dimensional output is based on the dendritic model of pyramidal
neurons, which can be characterized as deep, multilayer networks[Poirazi et al., 2003, Poirazi and
Papoutsi, 2020, Beniaguev et al., 2021].
    We also draw on a statistical property of cortical representations: task-relevant activity is
distributed across populations and partially redundant, rather than localized to single reliable units
[Georgopoulos et al., 1986, Averbeck et al., 2006, Cohen and Kohn, 2011], while individual cortical
neurons are variable across trials and only partially reliable [Tolhurst et al., 1983, Softky and Koch,
1993, Shadlen and Newsome, 1998, Faisal et al., 2008, Churchland et al., 2010, Goris et al., 2014].
Algorithmically, this motivates using a large population of small subnetworks whose contributions
are individually weak but heterogeneous, and whose training histories differ across tasks. A new task
can then be configured by selecting an assembly from many partially trained units, allowing useful
computation before any further weight update. In this view, transfer arises not from retrieving
a single stored expert, but from recombining a distributed population of imperfect, task-shaped

components.

Contributions. Our contribution is to study continual learning as a support-conditioned routing
problem: at each training and evaluation step, a small labeled support batch replaces explicit task
identity as the evidence used to infer which computation should be active. FTN implements this
idea by optimizing a sparse binary assembly mask over a large bank of independently parameterized
subnetworks while holding model weights fixed during configuration. This gives a single mechanism
for isolation and transfer: masks can separate tasks with incompatible input-output rules, but
can also overlap when the support set indicates reusable computation. Empirically, we show that
per-batch mask reconfiguration during training is the key ingredient: it creates a broad basis of
partially trained subnetworks that later tasks can recombine, producing lower pre-update head-start
loss than fixed parameter-isolation and retrieval baselines while retaining most of their protection
against forgetting. Across controlled concept- and domain-shift benchmarks, FTN therefore occupies
a middle regime between full sharing and disjoint task masks: it does not assume task identity, but
it does assume labeled functional evidence from the current task, and its goal is to convert that
evidence into task-appropriate reuse.


2    Related work
The first class of related models focus primarily on retention of prior solutions. Parameter-isolation
methods (PackNet [Mallya and Lazebnik, 2018], HAT [Serra et al., 2018], SupSup [Wortsman et al.,
2020]) assign tasks to disjoint parameter subsets; regularizers (EWC [Kirkpatrick et al., 2017],
Synaptic Intelligence [Zenke et al., 2017]) penalize drift on parameters important for earlier tasks;
replay methods (GEM/A-GEM [Lopez-Paz and Ranzato, 2017, Chaudhry et al., 2019], iCaRL
[Rebuffi et al., 2017], DER++) use stored exemplars. These target retention; they have no explicit
mechanism for forward transfer.
    Other methods target both retention and knowledge transfer. Progressive Networks [Rusu et al.,
2016] dedicate a fresh column per task with lateral connections to prior columns, attaining perfect
retention at linear-in-tasks parameter cost. PathNet [Fernando et al., 2017] searches paths through
a fixed module bank and reuses modules via path overlap, the closest conceptual analog to FTN.
Modular networks with task-driven priors [Veniat et al., 2021] make module-reuse-vs-grow explicit;
meta-learning approaches such as OML [Javed and White, 2019] target both axes through the
meta-objective. We position FTN inside this family: a parallel-bank architecture with a binary
support-set-derived mask that uses the same optimization to control isolation and transfer.
    Among knowledge transfer methods, the most relevant to FTN are those that use inference-time
support-set to determine the router state. Sparse expert models [Shazeer et al., 2017, Fedus et al.,
2022] and conditional channel-gated networks [Abati et al., 2020] learn gates over subnetworks. More
directly relevant, a family of methods uses a labeled support set to infer task identity or pick from a
bank of stored task models: supermask retrieval in SupSup [Wortsman et al., 2020], prompt-pool
selection in L2P and DualPrompt [Wang et al., 2022b,a], and learned routers on support-set features.
This is the central comparator class.
    Finally, Top-k routing requires optimization through a discrete constraint; we compare a sigmoid
relaxation, an annealed straight-through estimator [Bengio et al., 2013], and a Gumbel-Sigmoid
/ Concrete relaxation [Jang et al., 2017, Maddison et al., 2017] as three candidate methods for
determining the routing mask.

Architecture. An FTN contains H independent scalar-output “neurons”, i.e. subnetworks
zj = fj (x). A binary mask m ∈ {0, 1}H with ∥m∥0 = k selects an active subset of the networks,
and a linear readout produces
                                 ŷ = Wout (Dropout(z) ⊙ m).
     The model tested here uses H = 8192 neurons, each an MLP with L = 8 hidden layers of width
8, dropout p = 0.2. Of these, k = 512 neurons were active at a time. All architecturally-matched
baselines use the same parallel bank. The bank of neurons is deliberately wide relative to the
per-task budget. Any individual neuron is unreliable (it may have been trained on an unrelated task
or be inactive on the current input) so the configurer aggregates a sparse subset of noisy units. The
combinatorially large pool of partially trained subnetworks is what the reconfiguration inner loop
recombines into a transfer solution on a new task, rather than a small number of curated experts.
     A configurer C receives the current model and a labeled support batch and returns a hard
top-k mask after S inner steps with model weights fixed. We compare Sigmoid, AnnealSTE, and
Gumbel-Sigmoid forms; AnnealSTE with S=30 was chosen as the default after performing sweeps
across each of the three methods and values of S (full details in Appendix A).
     To train neurons shared across tasks, each step draws a (x, y, m) triple from a 200-slot buffer
filtered by mask overlap: a stored entry is admitted when its mask overlaps the current mask
moderately, ρ ∈ (0.2, 0.8]. Entries with ρ ≤ 0.2 share too little mass to interfere, while entries with
ρ > 0.8 are likely already trained by the current or proximal minibatches. Filtered replay therefore
spends replay supervision only on the overlapping parameters, targeting task interference directly,
while the disjoint complement is protected by mask gating. As a comparison, matched-memory
uniform-random sampling (NoMask + ER in Section 5.2) ablates the contribution of the filter.

FTN training and evaluation procedure. Algorithm 1 summarizes the full FTN training and
evaluation loop, combining the configurer, the mask-overlap-filtered replay, and the support-set-
driven evaluation protocol. The same configurer C is used at every training step and at evaluation
time; the only difference between training and evaluation is whether the model parameters θ receive
a gradient step.


4    Experiments
Training and evaluation. In each of the following experiments, tasks arrive in contiguous blocks.
Before every forward pass of FTN, its configurer selects a mask on the current minibatch, then the
model updates through that mask only, with one mask-filtered replay sample drawn as described
above. At evaluation, all models under comparison receive a small labeled support set from a
target task, and for evaluating retention and transfer, a disjoint held-out batch from the same task.
The task identity is never disclosed. The configurer selects a mask using the support set, and all
metrics are computed on the held-out batch. For FTN, we refer to this as the reconfig protocol. For
diagnostics only, we also report a stored protocol that reuses a saved training mask and therefore
acts as a task-identity oracle.

Comparator suite. Seven comparators run on the same parallel-MLP backbone and support-
set evaluation protocol. NoMask (naive sequential fine-tuning), NoMask + ER (uniform-random
replay buffer matched in capacity to FTN’s filtered buffer), and FixedMask (deterministic disjoint
per-task blocks, task identity at eval) are the full-sharing/replay/oracle-isolation baselines. SupSup

assumptions than the support-conditioned routing setting studied here. In particular, many assume
explicit task identity, fixed task boundaries, or architectures that do not naturally map onto FTN’s
large sparse-bank setting. Rather than presenting potentially misleading reimplementations under
mismatched constraints, we construct architecture-matched controls that isolate the underlying
mechanisms most relevant to FTN: replay-based full sharing (NoMask+ER), oracle parameter

PathNet, MNTDP, and L2P/DualPrompt require a non-MLP backbone and are deferred to the

    We report (1) accuracy (ACC) as the average classification accuracy across all tasks trained
block-sequentially, (2) forgetting measure (FM) [Chaudhry et al., 2019] i.e, the loss retroactively
contributed by new tasks, and (3) backward transfer (BWT) [Lopez-Paz and Ranzato, 2017] on the
lower-triangular performance matrix under the reconfig protocol; (4) head-start loss is the loss on a
held-out batch from a new task after support-set mask configuration but before any weight update
on that task, and lower values indicate stronger forward transfer. On the synthetic and shared-shuffle
MNIST families, where ground-truth overlap is known, rshare is the Pearson correlation between
off-diagonal entries of the row-normalized mask-overlap matrix and the ground-truth concept-sharing
matrix.

5.1    Synthetic concept sharing
The correlated synthetic family is the only benchmark where ground-truth concept overlap is
known by construction, so retention, forward transfer, and the recovered routing geometry can all
be checked against a defined target. Table 1 shows that unmasked sequential training collapses
(FM=0.396 for NoMask) and matched-memory random replay only partly recovers (ACC=0.65).
The four parameter-isolation methods reach FM=0 to within sample noise (by construction for
three; within 0.03 for FTN despite no provided task identities), so retention does not separate the
masked methods. The differences are on the other axes.

    ACC reflects how much each isolation strategy lets the model learn: SupSup at 0.768 is
bottlenecked by its frozen random backbone, Progressive and FixedMask reach the oracle’s ≈ 0.95
by allocating fresh trained capacity per task, and FTN sits a few points below (0.92) because its
bank weights are shared rather than partitioned.
    Head-start loss measures how well the active subnetwork is already configured for a new task
before any weight update (averaged across tasks in the sequence): FTN at 0.40 is below every other
comparator, including Progressive (1.42, ∼3.5×) and SupSup (0.66). Interestingly, FTN + Router
reproduces FTN’s number (0.394 vs. 0.399) while the same retrieval on top of FixedMask, SupSup,
or Progressive cannot. For the router version, which retrieved masks from an explicit memory, the
per-batch reconfiguration during training is what populates that memory with a large variety of
partly-trained overlapping subnetworks. It follows from these results that taking the arg min of
the loss over that bank is a viable but much more computationally expensive alternative to the
reconfiguration’s gradient-based search, which only used 30 steps.
    The no-replay ablation raises FM about seven-fold (to 0.195) and drops ACC to 0.78, which
localizes retention to the filtered-replay channel that protects shared neurons. Random per-batch
masks (FTN training pipeline, configurer replaced by a fresh random k-of-H mask each step) retain
and transfer substantially worse than FTN under correlated concept shift, with ACC dropping from
0.919 to 0.774, FM rising from 0.029 to 0.119, and head-start loss rising from 0.399 to 1.194, showing
that stochastic sparse-bank training plus replay is insufficient when tasks share latent components
and that the support-conditioned configurer is necessary to route around the resulting cross-task
mask collisions.

5.2        MNIST concept and domain shift
MNIST provides a non-synthetic validation that the transfer pattern is not an artifact of the
sinusoidal generator. Table 2 compresses the three variants. On shared-shuffle MNIST (concept
shift), random replay fails as predicted, NoMask and NoMask+ER collapse relative to the masked
methods and have severe forgetting, while FTN retains within 0.023 FM and has much lower
head-start loss than all isolation/retrieval baselines (0.562 vs. 2.309 for FixedMask and 8.330
for Progressive Networks; Progressive’s lateral inputs carry the old digit-to-label map, which
actively conflicts with the new permutation). On Permuted MNIST (non-smooth domain shift),
replay and isolation already retain well, but FTN still has the lowest head-start loss. On Rotated

6    Discussion
In this paper we presented a brain-inspired, unified approach to continual learning and knowledge
transfer. We compared our novel method on both synthetic tasks to conduct diagnostics and on
applied tasks to examine generality. In summary, we find that a distributed population of small
but deep networks with stochastic, low-dimensional outputs, akin to pyramidal neurons, can be
automatically recruited into sparse assemblies that both recover learned tasks and provide significant
advantage over random initialization on new tasks.

What FTN’s per-batch reconfiguration adds over retrieval and isolation baselines.
The ablative retrieval routers play two diagnostic roles. FixedMask + Router (parameter-isolation
training, arg min-loss retrieval at eval) matches the task-identity oracle on retention but pays a high
head-start cost because every retrieved block is fixed and untrained for a new task. FTN + Router
(FTN training, retrieval at eval) inherits FTN’s training-time benefit and produces a head-start
loss comparable to FTN itself, so the head-start advantage stems from training-time per-batch
reconfiguration that builds a broad basis of partially trained subnetworks rather than from the
inner-loop optimization at evaluation. The head-start gap between FTN and FixedMask + Router
is the central empirical signal, and it is largest on benchmarks whose tasks share transferable
structure and smallest where the input shift carries the least such structure (Permuted MNIST).
SupSup, Progressive, and FixedMask each pay a different transfer cost: SupSup is capped by
its frozen random features; Progressive’s columns are task-specialized, so its head-start is useful
only when smoothly-parameterized lateral transfer happens to apply (helpful on Rotated MNIST,
counterproductive on shared-shuffle); FTN’s single shared bank with overlapping training masks
delivers the lowest head-start on every benchmark. The cost is a modest accuracy and forgetting
gap to Progressive on the MNIST families, which closes as the support set grows.

What the random-per-batch-mask ablation isolates. Holding the FTN training pipeline
fixed and replacing the support-conditioned configurer with a fresh random k-of-H mask each
step splits the configurer’s role along input-shift type. Under correlated concept shift on the
synthetic family the configurer is necessary even for retention, because the bank’s atoms see colliding
gradients across tasks that share latent components and only the configurer routes around the
collisions. Under domain shift (Permuted MNIST, Rotated MNIST) random masks match FTN on
retention, so the distributed bank with replay handles input-shift retention without the configurer;
the configurer’s contribution there is confined to head-start. On shared-shuffle MNIST (concept


                                                  11
shift via component-shared label permutations) retention is nearly identical between FTN and
the random-mask ablation while head-start is far worse without the configurer, because the bank
absorbs the digit-label conflicts but only the configurer recovers a useful pre-update mask on a new
permutation. The Qwen LoRA-bank stream, where random masks match FTN-LoRA on retention
(Table 3), reads consistently with the domain-shift end of this picture: the six tasks span language,
label-space size, and domain rather than carrying correlated concept structure, so the configurer’s
specific role is backward transfer, not retention.

Neither replay nor masking are sufficient alone. Removing the mask-overlap-filtered buffer
markedly increases forgetting across every task family, with the largest jumps on the families
whose tasks share latent structure: the mask alone is partial protection. Conversely, replay alone
(NoMask + ER, matched memory) is sufficient under domain shift but collapses on shared-shuffle
MNIST, since concept shift presents contradictory replay targets on the same input. FTN’s
combination of mask-filtered replay and per-batch reconfiguration is what produces strong retention
and non-trivial transfer across all four families. High mean off-diagonal mask overlap occurs in both
correlated and orthogonal synthetic regimes (Appendix I); the diagnostic signal is the correlation
rshare between the model’s pairwise-overlap matrix and the ground-truth concept-sharing matrix,
not the absolute overlap level.

Limitations and outlook. The MLP suite with the random-mask control gives the cleanest
mechanism picture; the LoRA-bank experiment supports the retention half of that picture and
reads consistently with the domain-shift end of the axis. It is silent on head-start because the LLM
stream was selected for diversity rather than for measured cross-task structure. Future work should
construct an LLM-scale task family with measurable cross-task structure (e.g., Split CIFAR-100
superclasses on a vision backbone, or a synthetic LLM stream with controlled latent overlap),
sweep the LoRA B0 initialization scale, run a 7B/14B Qwen scale-up, and evaluate generative and
reasoning continual-LLM benchmarks under matched compute and memory.

A     Implementation details
Configurers. All three configurers expose the same external interface and differ in the soft-mask
form, optimizer, logit initialization, and auxiliary loss. AnnealSTE uses SGD, zero initialization,
hard k-WTA with a straight-through estimator, and no auxiliary term. Sigmoid uses RMSprop,
zero initialization, a soft mask σ(m), and post-hoc top-k projection. Gumbel-Sigmoid uses Adam,
initialization at σ −1 (k/H), σ((m + gi )/τi ) with logistic noise, and the budget penalty ( σ(m) −
                                                                                            P

k)2 /k 2 .

Replay buffer. The replay buffer stores 200 (x, y, m) triples. On every gradient step we sample
one triple whose stored mask has fractional overlap ρ = (mcur · mj )/∥mcur ∥1 ∈ (0.2, 0.8] with the
current mask. Entries with ρ ≤ 0.2 are skipped because they are unlikely to share useful computation
with the current update; entries with ρ > 0.8 are skipped because the current minibatch already
trains a very similar mask.

Reconfig protocol. At evaluation time we draw a labeled support set from the target task and
run the configurer on it to obtain a recall mask. The model is not told which task generated the
support set. Metrics are computed on a larger held-out batch from the same task, disjoint from the
support set.

SupSup baseline. We instantiate SupSup [Wortsman et al., 2020] on the same parallel-MLP
bank (H = 8192 slots, per-MLP topology identical to FTN’s). The bank’s parallel-MLP weights are
initialized once and frozen for the rest of training; dropout on the bank output is disabled for SupSup
so the frozen features are deterministic. For each task t we instantiate (i) a fresh score vector st ∈ RH
and (ii) a fresh per-task readout head Wt ∈ Rout×H initialized by Xavier-uniform. The forward pass
uses a hard top-k mask via the straight-through estimator mask = topk(st , k) + st − detach(st );
both st and Wt receive gradient through the task loss, the bank does not. Optimisers: Adam,
lr = 10−2 on st and 3 × 10−4 on Wt . Per-task storage is the pair (topk(st , k), Wt ). At evaluation
(no task identity) the support-set retrieval scans every stored pair, swaps in the candidate readout,
computes the support cross-entropy, and returns the lowest-loss pair; the held-out batch is then
evaluated under that pair. This is the deterministic, one-hot version of SupSup’s GG retrieval.

Progressive Networks baseline. We instantiate Progressive Networks [Rusu et al., 2016] on the
same parallel-MLP bank. Task t owns a disjoint k-block “column” covering slots [t · k, (t + 1) · k).
During training of task t: (i) the parallel-MLP weights for slots outside that range are frozen
(gradients zeroed before the optimizer step), so all prior columns are untouched; (ii) the active mask
is the cumulative mask mt = ⊮[ slot < (t + 1) · k ], so the per-task readout head Wt takes input
from the new column and all previously trained frozen columns (the lateral channel); (iii) only Wt
and the new-column slot weights receive gradient. Per-task storage is the pair (mt , Wt ) where mt is
the cumulative mask. At evaluation the same support-set retrieval as SupSup is used: scan stored
pairs, return the lowest-support-loss pair, evaluate held-out under it. Hyperparameters match FTN
(k = 512, Adam lr = 3 × 10−4 , no replay). For task counts up to ⌊H/k⌋ = 16 no slot wraps; the four
benchmarks here use 6, 10, 10, and 12 tasks respectively, so column allocation never collides. This
adaptation preserves the two defining properties of Progressive Networks (frozen prior capacity and
lateral access for forward transfer); the only deviations from Rusu et al. [2016] are that the columns
are sub-blocks of a single backbone (rather than separately allocated networks) and that we use
support-set retrieval at evaluation rather than task identity, since our protocol forbids task identity.

B     FTN-LoRA: B0 initialization, ablations, and the routing-vs-
      random control
This appendix documents two design choices for the LoRA-bank experiment in Section 5.3: (i) the
non-standard initialization B0 ∼ N (0, 0.01) in place of the usual B0 =0, and (ii) the random-per-
batch-mask ablation that isolates the contribution of the support-conditioned configurer from the
rest of the FTN-LoRA training pipeline. Table 4 reports the full ablation, including both diagnostic
B0 =0 runs and the headline five-seed results.

Standard B0 =0 initialization. Standard LoRA initializes A from a Kaiming-uniform distribution
and B to all zeros, so the adapter contributes nothing on step 0 and the fine-tuned model starts at
the base backbone exactly. We first ran FTN-LoRA with this initialization. Retention was already
reasonable (FTN-LoRA B0 =0 + Router reaches ACC 0.76 ± 0.04, FM 0.19 ± 0.02 across two seeds),
but the configurer’s head-start NLL was substantially worse than the retrieval baselines: 1.66 ± 0.17
(FTN B0 =0) and 1.95 ± 0.35 (FTN B0 =0 + Router) versus 1.22–1.32 for the matched-backbone
baselines. The configurer was producing masks that actively hurt the new task.

Diagnosis. With Bj exactly zero, atom j’s contribution to the bank output is xAj Bj = 0
irrespective of Aj , so ∂L/∂mj = 0. Under the AnnealSTE straight-through estimator, the gradient
on the mask logit for atom j is therefore also zero: the inner loop receives no signal about whether
atom j would help on the new task. It can deselect atoms whose previous-task training is actively
misaligned with the support set, but it cannot pull useful untrained atoms into the active set without
first selecting them by chance. The parallel-MLP backbone in Section 4 avoids this because every
parallel MLP, trained or not, contributes a stochastic non-zero output, so the configurer’s gradient
on the mask is informative about every atom from step 0.

B0 ∼ N (0, 0.01). We replace zero initialization with a small Gaussian, B0 ∼ N (0, 0.01). The model
still starts close to the base backbone (the per-atom contribution is small), but every atom produces
a non-zero output that gives the configurer a non-zero gradient signal on its mask logit. This
change unlocks the FTN-LoRA family results in Table 4: ACC rises from 0.62 to 0.83, FM falls
from 0.28 to 0.09, and BWT flips from −0.20 to +0.19, all within multi-seed sample noise of the
random-per-batch-mask ablation that shares the same training pipeline (next paragraph).

The random-per-batch-mask ablation. A reasonable concern is that the small non-zero B0
injects useful adapter perturbations on its own, and that the headline FTN-LoRA gains in Section 5.3
therefore reflect a non-FTN cause. We test this with a five-seed control: FTN-LoRA, random per-
batch masks keeps the FTN-LoRA training pipeline (mask-overlap-filtered replay, B0 ∼ N (0, 0.01),
the same 100-step schedule and active-parameter budget, the same per-batch reconfiguration cadence)
but replaces the support-conditioned configurer with a fresh random k-of-H mask each step. The
eval-time configurer is unchanged. The result decomposes the headline cleanly: the random-mask
ablation matches FTN-LoRA on retention (ACC 0.85 ± 0.12 vs 0.83 ± 0.10, FM 0.12 ± 0.07 vs
0.09 ± 0.09) and on head-start NLL (1.38 ± 0.18 vs 1.42 ± 0.25), so the training pipeline rather than
the configurer is the source of those numbers; but it does not flip backward transfer (−0.08 ± 0.12
vs +0.19 ± 0.10), so the support-conditioned configurer specifically is what produces FTN-LoRA’s
positive BWT. The FTN-LoRA mean lies more than one standard deviation above zero across
five seeds and the random-mask mean lies below zero, so the sign of BWT separates the two
configurations on this seed budget; we do not claim a paired-bootstrap significance test here, and

What this jointly establishes and what it does not. The two ablations together support a
narrower mechanism claim than the controlled MLP suite’s: at LoRA scale the support-conditioned
configurer is the load-bearing piece for backward transfer specifically, while the headline retention is
explained by the training pipeline (filtered replay, non-zero B0 , per-batch reconfiguration on a wide
bank). The LoRA experiment is silent on head-start: every method in Table 4 lands in the narrow
band 1.22–1.42, well within the per-row standard deviations of 0.15–0.25. Head-start can only vary
across methods if prior-task adapters carry usable bias for the new task, and the six-task stream
was selected for diversity (Chinese stance, English finance sentiment, English question type, English
emotion, English news topic, English ontology) rather than for measured cross-task structure: most
pairings cross both a language change and a label-space change, so even an oracle would have limited
prior-update structure to extract. The parallel-MLP suite avoids this confound by construction
(correlated synthetic and shared-shuffle MNIST have hand-tuned concept overlap), and remains
the appropriate probe of FTN’s head-start claim. BWT separates on the LoRA suite because
it depends on training-time bank dynamics across tasks rather than on any specific task pair’s
cross-task structure. What we have not done at LoRA scale: a B0 scale sweep, a Qwen-7B/14B
scale-up, an order-sensitivity check, or an LLM stream with measurable cross-task structure that
would let head-start adjudicate the configurer’s pre-update mechanism.

with α = 16 and dropout p = 0.05 applied to the bank input. The implementation contracts both
the per-atom and rank dimensions in a single einsum (...kr,kro→...o) so the per-atom output
dimension is never materialized; without this contraction the support-set inner loop’s activation
memory blows up at the large MLP intermediate dimension. Total bank parameter count is ≈ 591M
(≈ 39% of the backbone parameters).

Mask configurer. The same AnnealSTE configurer from Appendix A is used at the LoRA
bank’s slot dimension. Inner-loop count S = 3 (vs. S = 30 for the MLP suite); at the LLM scale
each inner-loop forward through the model is much more expensive, and we found three iterations
sufficient on the support batch. Logit learning rate is 0.5 with SGD, zero initialization. During
the inner loop we set requires_grad=False on the bank and disable gradient checkpointing on the
backbone (we do not need bank-weight or backbone-activation gradients; only the gradient with
respect to the mask logits). This keeps the configurer’s peak memory comparable to one ordinary
forward.

Continual-learning loop. Each task is trained for S=100 gradient steps at batch size 4 (no
gradient accumulation), sequence length 384, AdamW with lr = 2×10−4 and no weight decay on
the bank. For FTN-LoRA, every training step runs the configurer on a fresh 4-example support
sub-batch from the current task to obtain the active mask, then takes a single gradient step on the
query batch through the active atoms. The mask-overlap-filtered replay buffer holds 200 (x, y, m)
triples; on every 10-th step a random current-task example is added with its current mask, and on
every step a replay sample is drawn whose stored mask has fractional overlap ρ ∈ (0.1, 0.7] with
the current mask. Gradients on the query and replay batches are summed before the AdamW step.
SeqLoRA + ER uses a uniform-random buffer of the same capacity and a single replay sample per
step.

Task suite and prompt format. Six tasks are presented in canonical order: C-STANCE-
A [Zhao et al., 2024] (Chinese stance detection, three-class: Support / Against / Neutral), Financial
PhraseBank [Malo et al., 2014] (English finance sentiment, three-class), TREC [Li and Roth,
2002] (English question type, six-class), dair-ai/emotion [Saravia et al., 2018] (English emotion,
six-class), AG News [Zhang et al., 2015] (English topic, four-class), DBpedia 14 [Zhang et al.,
2015] (English ontology, fourteen-class). The suite is selected to span heterogeneity along language
(Chinese and English), label-space size (2 to 14 classes), and domain (social-media stance, finance,
question-type taxonomy, emotion lexicon, news topic, ontology). It is an assembled continual-
classification benchmark, not a replication of any prior named benchmark. Per task we use 400
training examples, 100 evaluation examples, and a fixed 16-example labeled support set; train
and eval are stratified across labels for class-balanced sampling. Every example is wrapped in the
Qwen2.5 chat template with a fixed system message and the loss is masked to the answer tokens
(the prompt receives no gradient signal).

Metrics. We report final accuracy (ACC), forgetting measure (FM), and backward transfer (BWT)
on the lower-triangular performance matrix R[i, j] with the same definitions as Section 4. Accuracy
is the exact-match accuracy on the first answer token; head-start NLL is the answer-token negative
log likelihood on a held-out batch, computed with the configurer’s selected mask (or the method’s
eval-time mask for retrieval baselines) before any gradient update on the new task. We use 64
held-out examples for head-start. Five seeds per method; the controlled MLP results in Section 4
(5–8 seeds across the same comparator family) provide the broader reliability claim, and the LoRA
per-method standard deviations (Table 3) are accordingly indicative rather than tight confidence
intervals.

Compute footprint. Each method completes the six-task sequence in 4–8 minutes (non-FTN
methods) or 25–30 minutes (FTN-LoRA family, which adds S = 3 inner-loop forward/backward
passes through the model per training step) on a single 80 GiB H100. Eight methods × one seed
therefore fit on eight GPUs in parallel.

E                   Secondary Experiments
E.1                     Primary search and baselines
We first sweep configurer type in {Sigmoid, AnnealSTE, Gumbel-Sigmoid} and inner-step budget
S ∈ {10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60} on the correlated synthetic family with k = 512, support size 256, and
per-batch reconfiguration. The sweep uses one shared seed to compare the 18 configurations within
seed; subsequent ablations report mean and standard deviation over 8 seeds. All three configurers
reach low forgetting (FM in [0.04, 0.06] across the sweep) and similar accuracy (ACC in [0.89, 0.92]).
The discriminating axis is head-start: AnnealSTE attains the best head-start loss at every budget
(mean 0.37 across S, vs. 0.46 for Sigmoid and 0.55 for Gumbel-Sigmoid) and is stable across budgets,
while Gumbel-Sigmoid improves steadily with S but does not reach AnnealSTE’s level. AnnealSTE
at S = 30 gives head-start 0.358, FM = 0.049, rshare = 0.509; doubling the budget to S = 60 yields
no head-start gain (0.386) at twice the inner-loop cost. We therefore adopt AnnealSTE, S = 30,
k = 512, support size 256, and per-batch reconfiguration as the default. The full 3 × 6 sweep table
is in Appendix F; Figure 5 plots head-start and forgetting against budget.
    Tables 6 and 7 compare FTN with two endpoints using the same parallel bank. NoMask trains
the full bank on every task, representing full sharing. FixedMask assigns deterministic disjoint
blocks of k neurons, representing oracle isolation with no mask inference or sharing. FTN lies
between these endpoints. On synthetic tasks, NoMask has FM = 0.392 ± 0.016, FixedMask has
0 by construction, and FTN obtains 0.055 ± 0.015, closing about 86% of the forgetting gap. On
shared-shuffle MNIST, FTN closes about 97% of the gap. Unlike FixedMask, FTN also improves
head-start loss on correlated tasks and recovers nonzero concept-sharing structure.

E.3                        Sparsity and support-set size
