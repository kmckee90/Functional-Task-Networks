Fully Autonomous Continual Learning and Knowledge Transfer via Cortex-Inspired Neural Assemblies

Kevin L. McKee, Yicong Zheng, Zacharie Bugaud, Thomas Miconi
Astera Institute


ABSTRACT

Continual learning requires both parameter isolation, to protect task-specific computation, and parameter reuse, to transfer useful computation to later tasks. We introduce Functional Task Networks (FTNs), a unified mechanism of continual learning and knowledge transfer modeled after several properties of the mammalian neocortex. An FTN is formulated as a large, distributed, stochastic mixture of experts in which a sparse routing mask is computed at inference time over a small support sample of input-output pairs. Per-batch reconfiguration during training, together with mask-overlap-filtered replay, shapes a basis of overlapping, partially trained subnetworks. Unrelated tasks route through mostly separate assemblies, related tasks overlap on shared components. On block-sequentially trained continual learning and knowledge transfer tasks — including shuffled MNIST for concept shift, Permuted MNIST for domain shift, and Rotated MNIST for knowledge transfer — FTN retains solutions nearly as well as disjoint-mask isolation and attains the lowest pre-update head-start loss among matched-architecture comparators. The learned mask-overlap geometry tracks the known concept-sharing structure where available. We show experimentally that under correlated concept shift, support-set mask reconfiguration is necessary even for retention. Under domain shift, the distributed bank with replay is sufficient to handle retention without the configurer. Forward transfer (head-start) and backward transfer are also contributed by support-set mask configuration. We further implement FTN-LoRA, a bank of Low Rank Adaptation modules on a frozen Qwen2.5-1.5B backbone, and test it over a six-task multilingual classification stream, demonstrating minimal forgetting and positive backward transfer in a scaled setting.


1. INTRODUCTION

For continual learning in neural networks, shared parameters let a new task reuse representations learned from earlier tasks, but the same shared parameters can be overwritten by later gradients, producing catastrophic forgetting. Parameter isolation methods protect earlier computations by assigning tasks to separate subnetworks, but isolation weakens forward transfer because new tasks cannot easily recruit useful structure from old ones. A central question for general continual learning solutions is therefore how a model might automatically modulate between isolating and sharing its parameters.

Concept and domain shift. Continual-learning benchmarks differ in WHAT changes between tasks. Under domain shift the input distribution changes while the labeling rule remains broadly compatible. The most generic way to prevent forgetting under domain shift is shuffled replay-based methods, in which data from previous tasks are mixed into training minibatches. The gradients over the tasks are averaged, so no single task dominates all of the weight updates. Under concept shift the labeling rule or output function itself changes, so the same input must be mapped to different targets in different tasks. Methods like Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) and Synaptic Intelligence address domain shift but not concept shift: penalizing drift on parameters that NEED to compute different functions on different tasks only blocks learning. Naive replay similarly fails because two replayed labels for the same input contradict each other unless replay is routed through different subnetworks.

Block-sequential training. Block-sequential training presents a more difficult regime for retention than i.i.d. minibatching: each long stretch of updates is dominated by one task at a time, intensifying forgetting on other tasks. It is also the schedule of most realistic continual deployments (sequential adapter installation, on-the-job fine-tuning, sliding-window streams). We specifically target this training regime as a necessary part of the continual learning problem definition.

Functional Task Networks. We introduce Functional Task Networks (FTNs), a support-set-conditioned routing mechanism for continual learning without task identity. FTN uses a large bank of small but deep networks to learn modular but distributed task representations. During both training and inference, FTN selects a sparse assembly for each minibatch by optimizing a binary top-k mask to minimize loss with respect to a small labeled support set. This gives a single mechanism for both parameter isolation and reuse: unrelated tasks can be routed through mostly different assemblies, while related tasks can overlap on components that carry useful computation. We evaluate whether this mechanism produces the intended trade-off: retention under block-sequential training and concept shift, forward transfer before any update on a new task, and mask-overlap structure that reflects task relatedness when such structure is known. We show that FTN occupies a middle regime between full parameter sharing and fixed disjoint masks, retaining most of the protection of isolation while enabling recombination that fixed task masks cannot provide.

Biological motivation. FTN is motivated by several features of neocortical computation that suggest a route to sparse, reusable, and partially overlapping task representations. Sparsification via top-k masking has previously served as an abstraction for the effects of inhibitory competition among neurons. Our choice to use a large number of deep networks with low-dimensional output is based on the dendritic model of pyramidal neurons, which can be characterized as deep, multilayer networks. We also draw on a statistical property of cortical representations: task-relevant activity is distributed across populations and partially redundant, rather than localized to single reliable units, while individual cortical neurons are variable across trials and only partially reliable. Algorithmically, this motivates using a large population of small subnetworks whose contributions are individually weak but heterogeneous, and whose training histories differ across tasks. A new task can then be configured by selecting an assembly from many partially trained units, allowing useful computation before any further weight update. In this view, transfer arises not from retrieving a single stored expert, but from recombining a distributed population of imperfect, task-shaped components.

Contributions. Our contribution is to study continual learning as a support-conditioned routing problem: at each training and evaluation step, a small labeled support batch replaces explicit task identity as the evidence used to infer which computation should be active. FTN implements this idea by optimizing a sparse binary assembly mask over a large bank of independently parameterized subnetworks while holding model weights fixed during configuration. This gives a single mechanism for isolation and transfer: masks can separate tasks with incompatible input-output rules, but can also overlap when the support set indicates reusable computation. Empirically, per-batch mask reconfiguration during training is the key ingredient: it creates a broad basis of partially trained subnetworks that later tasks can recombine, producing lower pre-update head-start loss than fixed parameter-isolation and retrieval baselines while retaining most of their protection against forgetting. Across controlled concept- and domain-shift benchmarks, FTN therefore occupies a middle regime between full sharing and disjoint task masks: it does not assume task identity, but it does assume labeled functional evidence from the current task, and its goal is to convert that evidence into task-appropriate reuse.


2. RELATED WORK

The first class of related models focuses primarily on retention of prior solutions. Parameter-isolation methods (PackNet, HAT, SupSup) assign tasks to disjoint parameter subsets; regularizers (EWC, Synaptic Intelligence) penalize drift on parameters important for earlier tasks; replay methods (GEM/A-GEM, iCaRL, DER++) use stored exemplars. These target retention; they have no explicit mechanism for forward transfer.

Other methods target both retention and knowledge transfer. Progressive Networks dedicate a fresh column per task with lateral connections to prior columns, attaining perfect retention at linear-in-tasks parameter cost. PathNet searches paths through a fixed module bank and reuses modules via path overlap, the closest conceptual analog to FTN. Modular networks with task-driven priors (MNTDP) make module-reuse-vs-grow explicit; meta-learning approaches such as OML target both axes through the meta-objective. We position FTN inside this family: a parallel-bank architecture with a binary support-set-derived mask that uses the same optimization to control isolation and transfer.

Among knowledge transfer methods, the most relevant to FTN are those that use an inference-time support set to determine the router state. Sparse expert models and conditional channel-gated networks learn gates over subnetworks. More directly relevant, a family of methods uses a labeled support set to infer task identity or pick from a bank of stored task models: supermask retrieval in SupSup, prompt-pool selection in L2P and DualPrompt, and learned routers on support-set features. This is the central comparator class.

Finally, top-k routing requires optimization through a discrete constraint; we compare a sigmoid relaxation, an annealed straight-through estimator, and a Gumbel-Sigmoid / Concrete relaxation as three candidate methods for determining the routing mask.


3. METHOD

Architecture. An FTN contains H independent scalar-output "neurons", i.e. subnetworks z_j = f_j(x). A binary mask m in {0,1}^H with exactly k nonzero entries (L0 norm = k) selects an active subset of the networks, and a linear readout produces

    y_hat = W_out (Dropout(z) * m),

where * is elementwise gating by the mask. The model tested here uses H = 8192 neurons, each an MLP with L = 8 hidden layers of width 8, dropout p = 0.2. Of these, k = 512 neurons are active at a time. All architecturally-matched baselines use the same parallel bank. The bank of neurons is deliberately wide relative to the per-task budget. Any individual neuron is unreliable (it may have been trained on an unrelated task or be inactive on the current input), so the configurer aggregates a sparse subset of noisy units. The combinatorially large pool of partially trained subnetworks is what the reconfiguration inner loop recombines into a transfer solution on a new task, rather than a small number of curated experts.

A configurer C receives the current model and a labeled support batch and returns a hard top-k mask after S inner steps with model weights fixed. We compare Sigmoid, AnnealSTE, and Gumbel-Sigmoid forms; AnnealSTE with S = 30 was chosen as the default after sweeps across each of the three methods and values of S.

To train neurons shared across tasks, each step draws an (x, y, m) triple from a 200-slot buffer filtered by mask overlap: a stored entry is admitted when its mask overlaps the current mask moderately, with fractional overlap rho in (0.2, 0.8]. Entries with rho <= 0.2 share too little mass to interfere, while entries with rho > 0.8 are likely already trained by the current or proximal minibatches. Filtered replay therefore spends replay supervision only on the overlapping parameters, targeting task interference directly, while the disjoint complement is protected by mask gating. As a comparison, matched-memory uniform-random sampling (the NoMask + ER baseline) ablates the contribution of the filter.

FTN training and evaluation procedure. The full FTN training and evaluation loop combines the configurer, the mask-overlap-filtered replay, and the support-set-driven evaluation protocol. The same configurer C is used at every training step and at evaluation time; the only difference between training and evaluation is whether the model parameters theta receive a gradient step.

Algorithm (Functional Task Networks: training and reconfig evaluation).
Inputs: tasks T_1..T_T arriving block-sequentially; a bank of H subnetworks {f_j} with parameters theta; readout W_out; active budget k; configurer C with S inner steps; replay buffer B of capacity N_B; replay overlap window (rho_lo, rho_hi]; support size n_s.
  - Initialize theta, W_out, and an empty buffer B.
  - For each task t = 1..T:
      - Reset optimizer state for (theta, W_out).
      - For each training minibatch (x, y) from T_t:
          - Draw a support batch (x_s, y_s) from T_t of size n_s.
          - Mask configuration with theta frozen: initialize mask logits m_0; for s = 1..S, m_s = configurer step C(m_{s-1}; x_s, y_s, theta); then m_cur = TopK(m_S, k) in {0,1}^H.
          - Mask-overlap-filtered replay: sample (x_r, y_r, m_r) uniformly from buffer entries whose stored mask has (m' . m_cur)/k in (rho_lo, rho_hi].
          - Gradient step through the active mask, current plus replay:
              L = loss(W_out(z(x) * m_cur), y) + loss(W_out(z(x_r) * m_r), y_r).
          - Update (theta, W_out) with the gradient of L.
          - Optionally insert (x, y, m_cur) into B (evict oldest if full).
  - Evaluation (reconfig protocol): given any target task t* and an unlabeled query, draw a labeled support batch from T_{t*}, run the configurer for S inner steps with theta frozen to obtain mask m*, and predict y_hat = W_out(z(x) * m*). Task identity is never disclosed.


4. EXPERIMENTS

Training and evaluation. In each experiment, tasks arrive in contiguous blocks. Before every forward pass of FTN, its configurer selects a mask on the current minibatch, then the model updates through that mask only, with one mask-filtered replay sample drawn as described above. At evaluation, all models under comparison receive a small labeled support set from a target task and, for evaluating retention and transfer, a disjoint held-out batch from the same task. The task identity is never disclosed. The configurer selects a mask using the support set, and all metrics are computed on the held-out batch. For FTN, we refer to this as the reconfig protocol. For diagnostics only, we also report a stored protocol that reuses a saved training mask and therefore acts as a task-identity oracle.

Comparator suite. Seven comparators run on the same parallel-MLP backbone and support-set evaluation protocol. NoMask (naive sequential fine-tuning), NoMask + ER (uniform-random replay buffer matched in capacity to FTN's filtered buffer), and FixedMask (deterministic disjoint per-task blocks, task identity at eval) are the full-sharing / replay / oracle-isolation baselines. SupSup freezes a randomly initialized parallel bank and learns a per-task score vector and readout, evaluated by support-set retrieval over stored masks. Progressive Networks use disjoint per-task k-blocks on the same bank with old columns frozen and lateral access from prior columns into the new readout, evaluated by support-set retrieval over the stored cumulative-column masks. FixedMask + Router is the simpler PackNet-style retrieval baseline; FTN + Router (FTN training, retrieval at eval) asks whether the FTN inner loop beats simple retrieval over the FTN-trained bank.

Several standard continual-learning methods (EWC, SI, LwF, GEM/A-GEM, DER++, HAT, PackNet) optimize primarily for retention under different architectural and evaluation assumptions than the support-conditioned routing setting studied here. Many assume explicit task identity, fixed task boundaries, or architectures that do not naturally map onto FTN's large sparse-bank setting. Rather than presenting potentially misleading reimplementations under mismatched constraints, we construct architecture-matched controls that isolate the mechanisms most relevant to FTN: replay-based full sharing (NoMask + ER), oracle parameter isolation (FixedMask), and support-set retrieval over isolated subnetworks (FixedMask + Router). PathNet, MNTDP, and L2P/DualPrompt require a non-MLP backbone and are deferred to the LoRA-bank instantiation.

Benchmarks and metrics. A class-label generating function of arbitrary difficulty for artificial neural networks can be generated by composing sinusoids of random input-conditioned frequencies. The synthetic task family has T = 12 binary classification tasks in d = 2, generated from a fixed-encoder sinusoidal mixture over C = 6 latent concept components: x ~ Normal(0, I_d); z_tilde(x; t) = sin(f (W_enc x * m_t)) * m_t; y(x; t) = indicator[sum_l z_tilde_l > 0], with f = 8 and m_t in {0,1}^L a binary task-component selector. The correlated variant has m_t select two of C = 6 components (one of the 15 possible pairs, uniquely per task), so each component is shared by exactly two tasks; the orthogonal variant uses C = 2T = 24 components partitioned into disjoint pairs (zero off-diagonal sharing). Three example tasks from the correlated synthetic family (Figure: tasks) show binary decision surfaces over the 2-D input x, points colored by class y; tasks share latent components, so boundaries share gross structure but differ in orientation and high-frequency interior — the concept overlap FTN's mask geometry is designed to recover.

The MNIST suite spans concept and domain shift: shared-shuffle MNIST (6 tasks) gives each task a digit-to-label permutation that is the union of two component sub-permutations, exposing ground-truth concept overlap; Permuted MNIST and Rotated MNIST (10 tasks each) are domain-incremental, applying a fixed random pixel permutation or a fixed rotation in equal steps from 0 to 180 degrees. Each MLP cell trains 1,000 Adam steps per task at learning rate 3e-4, train minibatch size 256, and optimizer-state reset at task boundaries.

We report: (1) accuracy (ACC), the average classification accuracy across all tasks trained block-sequentially; (2) forgetting measure (FM), the loss retroactively contributed by new tasks; (3) backward transfer (BWT) on the lower-triangular performance matrix under the reconfig protocol; and (4) head-start loss, the loss on a held-out batch from a new task after support-set mask configuration but before any weight update on that task — lower values indicate stronger forward transfer. On the synthetic and shared-shuffle MNIST families, where ground-truth overlap is known, r_share is the Pearson correlation between off-diagonal entries of the row-normalized mask-overlap matrix and the ground-truth concept-sharing matrix.


5. RESULTS

5.1 Synthetic concept sharing

The correlated synthetic family is the only benchmark where ground-truth concept overlap is known by construction, so retention, forward transfer, and the recovered routing geometry can all be checked against a defined target. Unmasked sequential training collapses (FM = 0.396 for NoMask) and matched-memory random replay only partly recovers (ACC = 0.65). The four parameter-isolation methods reach FM = 0 to within sample noise (by construction for three; within 0.03 for FTN despite no provided task identities), so retention does not separate the masked methods. The differences are on the other axes.

(Table, correlated synthetic family, summary of the comparator results: NoMask collapses with FM about 0.396; NoMask + ER recovers to ACC about 0.65; SupSup reaches ACC 0.768, bottlenecked by its frozen random backbone; Progressive Networks and FixedMask reach the oracle's ACC of about 0.95 by allocating fresh trained capacity per task; FTN reaches ACC about 0.92 with FM about 0.029-0.055 and head-start loss about 0.40, the lowest of any comparator. FTN + Router reproduces FTN's head-start, about 0.394 vs 0.399, while retrieval on top of FixedMask, SupSup, or Progressive cannot. Removing replay raises FM roughly sevenfold to about 0.195 and drops ACC to 0.78; replacing the configurer with fresh random per-batch masks drops ACC from 0.919 to 0.774, raises FM from 0.029 to 0.119, and raises head-start from 0.399 to 1.194.)

ACC reflects how much each isolation strategy lets the model learn: SupSup at 0.768 is bottlenecked by its frozen random backbone, Progressive and FixedMask reach the oracle's about 0.95 by allocating fresh trained capacity per task, and FTN sits a few points below (0.92) because its bank weights are shared rather than partitioned.

Head-start loss measures how well the active subnetwork is already configured for a new task before any weight update (averaged across tasks in the sequence): FTN at 0.40 is below every other comparator, including Progressive (1.42, about 3.5x higher) and SupSup (0.66). Interestingly, FTN + Router reproduces FTN's number (0.394 vs 0.399) while the same retrieval on top of FixedMask, SupSup, or Progressive cannot. For the router version, which retrieved masks from an explicit memory, the per-batch reconfiguration during TRAINING is what populates that memory with a large variety of partly-trained overlapping subnetworks. It follows that taking the argmin of the loss over that bank is a viable but much more computationally expensive alternative to the reconfiguration's gradient-based search, which only used 30 steps.

The no-replay ablation raises FM about sevenfold (to 0.195) and drops ACC to 0.78, which localizes retention to the filtered-replay channel that protects shared neurons. Random per-batch masks (FTN training pipeline, configurer replaced by a fresh random k-of-H mask each step) retain and transfer substantially worse than FTN under correlated concept shift, with ACC dropping from 0.919 to 0.774, FM rising from 0.029 to 0.119, and head-start loss rising from 0.399 to 1.194 — showing that stochastic sparse-bank training plus replay is insufficient when tasks share latent components, and that the support-conditioned configurer is necessary to route around the resulting cross-task mask collisions.

16-task retention and transfer. We contrast the unmasked control (no routing, full network capacity exposed to every task) with the full FTN method on the same task family, same seed, and otherwise identical pipeline. Both are 16x16 task-vs-task evaluation matrices obtained by training each model sequentially on 16 orthogonal tasks from the synthetic data generator and then evaluating the resulting model on each task's held-out batch at every intermediate checkpoint. (Figure: the null result, with no masking and full network capacity, shows high accuracy only on the diagonal — the model performs well only on the task it has just finished training, with no retention in the lower triangle and no forward transfer in the upper triangle. The full FTN method on the same 16-task sequence shows a lower triangle with uniformly high scores close to the diagonal, i.e. near-perfect retention of every prior task after later tasks have been learned, and an upper triangle substantially better than the null result, showing large forward transfer that improves as more tasks are introduced.) The tasks are generated to be orthogonal (not sharing data-generating components), yet the null result shows some chance correlation between tasks, and the full result shows the model can exploit such chance correlations in its forward knowledge transfer. Where task-invariant representations did not exist, the distributed and stochastic training of the network produced a large repertoire of partially-trained task bases that nonetheless allowed rapid construction of solutions on each new task.

Sparsity, support size, and the orthogonal control. Three diagnostic sweeps: (i) The top-k budget controls how much overlap is possible: sweeping k in {512, 128, 32, 8} reveals an isolation-transfer trade-off. FM falls from 0.056 at k = 512 to 0.032 at k = 32 while head-start rises from about 0.42 to 0.60; sharing fidelity peaks at moderate sparsity (r_share = 0.624 at k = 32). (ii) Support-set size affects transfer more than retention: shrinking the support from 256 to 4 raises head-start from 0.425 +/- 0.080 to 1.339 +/- 0.095 while FM stays at or below 0.060 and ACC at 0.86-0.89. (iii) The orthogonal variant, which removes latent sharing while preserving the task format, raises FTN head-start from 0.423 +/- 0.077 to 0.568 +/- 0.045 with low forgetting in both regimes; r_share is zero by construction for the orthogonal task family.

Recovered sharing geometry. Comparing the ground-truth sharing matrix with the row-normalized neuron-sharing matrix for a high-fidelity AnnealSTE configuration (S = 40, r_share = 0.617; the primary sweep reaches 0.622), the recovered matrix is not binary, but its high-overlap regions align with the concept-sharing pattern. BWT stays close to zero across the 18 primary-search configurations, ranging from -0.061 to +0.005, against -0.392 for NoMask on synthetic and -0.656 on MNIST. 5.2 MNIST concept and domain shift

MNIST provides a non-synthetic validation that the transfer pattern is not an artifact of the sinusoidal generator. On shared-shuffle MNIST (concept shift), random replay fails as predicted: NoMask and NoMask + ER collapse relative to the masked methods and have severe forgetting, while FTN retains within 0.023 FM and has much lower head-start loss than all isolation/retrieval baselines (0.562 vs 2.309 for FixedMask and 8.330 for Progressive Networks; Progressive's lateral inputs carry the old digit-to-label map, which actively conflicts with the new permutation). On Permuted MNIST (non-smooth domain shift), replay and isolation already retain well, but FTN still has the lowest head-start loss. On Rotated MNIST (smooth domain shift), Progressive comes close (0.381 vs FTN's 0.324), the expected case where smooth lateral transfer is useful. Across all three MNIST families, FTN gives up a small amount of final accuracy to Progressive/FixedMask but has the lowest head-start loss among the matched-backbone comparators. The FTN-with-random-per-batch-masks ablation holds the FTN training pipeline fixed (filtered replay, same step budget, same parameter budget) and replaces the support-conditioned configurer with a fresh random k-of-H mask each step. This ablation retains nearly as well as FTN on all three MNIST variants (about 0.92 / 0.92 / 0.87 ACC) but pays a head-start cost that depends on input-shift type (about 12x worse on shared-shuffle MNIST, 6.65 vs 0.56; about 2x worse on Permuted, 3.41 vs 1.62; and on Rotated, 0.62 vs 0.32).

5.3 Qwen2.5 LoRA-bank experiment

We also instantiate the same routing mechanism as a bank of LoRA atoms on a frozen Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct backbone. Each banked projection has H = 128 rank-4 atoms and uses k = 16 active atoms per forward pass, matching the active-parameter budget of the baselines. The six-task continual-classification stream is an assembled benchmark of public Hugging Face datasets covering Chinese and English text, six label-space sizes from 2 to 14, and five distinct domains: C-STANCE-A (Chinese stance), Financial PhraseBank (English finance sentiment), TREC (English question type), dair-ai/emotion, AG News, and DBpedia-14. We compare against four matched-backbone baselines plus one decisive ablation, FTN-LoRA random per-batch masks, which keeps FTN-LoRA's training pipeline (filtered replay, B_0 ~ Normal(0, 0.01), per-batch reconfiguration on the same step budget) but replaces the support-conditioned configurer with a fresh random k-of-H mask each step. This isolates the configurer's contribution from the rest of the pipeline.

FTN's training pipeline (filtered replay, non-zero B_0, per-batch reconfiguration on a wide bank) accounts for almost all of the gap to the parameter-isolation comparators on retention: FTN-LoRA reaches ACC 0.83 +/- 0.10 and FM 0.09 +/- 0.09 against 0.39-0.75 ACC and 0.15-0.55 FM in the baselines, and the random-per-batch-mask ablation lands at the same retention level (0.85 +/- 0.12 / 0.12 +/- 0.07). Taking the random-mask ablation into account, the support-conditioned mask configurer accounts for the greatest backward transfer: FTN-LoRA shows the only positive BWT in the table (+0.19 +/- 0.10), the random-mask ablation falls to -0.08 +/- 0.12, and the four matched-backbone baselines cluster between -0.47 and -0.09. The FTN-LoRA mean is more than one standard deviation above zero across five seeds and the random-mask mean is below zero, so the sign of BWT separates the two configurations across the seed budget.

Head-start NLL appeared uninformative on this suite, as every method lands in the narrow band 1.22-1.42, well within per-row standard deviations of 0.15-0.25. Head-start can only vary across methods if prior-task adapters carry usable bias for the new task, and this six-task stream was selected for diversity rather than for known cross-task structure. The random-mask ablation shows that retention parity between FTN and random masks is the signature of domain-shift without concept-shift, matching Permuted MNIST and Rotated MNIST in the controlled suite. We therefore read the LoRA stream as a heterogeneous domain-shift sequence rather than a correlated-concept-shift regime where the configurer would be necessary even for retention.


6. DISCUSSION

In this paper we presented a brain-inspired, unified approach to continual learning and knowledge transfer. We compared our novel method on synthetic tasks for diagnostics and on applied tasks to examine generality. In summary, we find that a distributed population of small but deep networks with stochastic, low-dimensional outputs, akin to pyramidal neurons, can be automatically recruited into sparse assemblies that both recover learned tasks and provide significant advantage over random initialization on new tasks.

What FTN's per-batch reconfiguration adds over retrieval and isolation baselines. The ablative retrieval routers play two diagnostic roles. FixedMask + Router (parameter-isolation training, argmin-loss retrieval at eval) matches the task-identity oracle on retention but pays a high head-start cost because every retrieved block is fixed and untrained for a new task. FTN + Router (FTN training, retrieval at eval) inherits FTN's training-time benefit and produces a head-start loss comparable to FTN itself, so the head-start advantage stems from training-time per-batch reconfiguration that builds a broad basis of partially trained subnetworks rather than from the inner-loop optimization at evaluation. The head-start gap between FTN and FixedMask + Router is the central empirical signal, and it is largest on benchmarks whose tasks share transferable structure and smallest where the input shift carries the least such structure (Permuted MNIST). SupSup, Progressive, and FixedMask each pay a different transfer cost: SupSup is capped by its frozen random features; Progressive's columns are task-specialized, so its head-start is useful only when smoothly-parameterized lateral transfer happens to apply (helpful on Rotated MNIST, counterproductive on shared-shuffle); FTN's single shared bank with overlapping training masks delivers the lowest head-start on every benchmark. The cost is a modest accuracy and forgetting gap to Progressive on the MNIST families, which closes as the support set grows.

What the random-per-batch-mask ablation isolates. Holding the FTN training pipeline fixed and replacing the support-conditioned configurer with a fresh random k-of-H mask each step splits the configurer's role along input-shift type. Under correlated concept shift on the synthetic family the configurer is necessary even for retention, because the bank's atoms see colliding gradients across tasks that share latent components and only the configurer routes around the collisions. Under domain shift (Permuted MNIST, Rotated MNIST) random masks match FTN on retention, so the distributed bank with replay handles input-shift retention without the configurer; the configurer's contribution there is confined to head-start. On shared-shuffle MNIST (concept shift via component-shared label permutations) retention is nearly identical between FTN and the random-mask ablation while head-start is far worse without the configurer, because the bank absorbs the digit-label conflicts but only the configurer recovers a useful pre-update mask on a new permutation. The Qwen LoRA-bank stream, where random masks match FTN-LoRA on retention, reads consistently with the domain-shift end of this picture: the six tasks span language, label-space size, and domain rather than carrying correlated concept structure, so the configurer's specific role is backward transfer, not retention.

Neither replay nor masking are sufficient alone. Removing the mask-overlap-filtered buffer markedly increases forgetting across every task family, with the largest jumps on the families whose tasks share latent structure: the mask alone is partial protection. Conversely, replay alone (NoMask + ER, matched memory) is sufficient under domain shift but collapses on shared-shuffle MNIST, since concept shift presents contradictory replay targets on the same input. FTN's combination of mask-filtered replay and per-batch reconfiguration is what produces strong retention and non-trivial transfer across all four families. High mean off-diagonal mask overlap occurs in both correlated and orthogonal synthetic regimes; the diagnostic signal is the correlation r_share between the model's pairwise-overlap matrix and the ground-truth concept-sharing matrix, not the absolute overlap level.

Limitations and outlook. The MLP suite with the random-mask control gives the cleanest mechanism picture; the LoRA-bank experiment supports the retention half of that picture and reads consistently with the domain-shift end of the axis. It is silent on head-start because the LLM stream was selected for diversity rather than for measured cross-task structure. Future work should construct an LLM-scale task family with measurable cross-task structure (e.g., Split CIFAR-100 superclasses on a vision backbone, or a synthetic LLM stream with controlled latent overlap), sweep the LoRA B_0 initialization scale, run a 7B/14B Qwen scale-up, and evaluate generative and reasoning continual-LLM benchmarks under matched compute and memory.


APPENDIX A. IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS

Configurers. All three configurers expose the same external interface and differ in the soft-mask form, optimizer, logit initialization, and auxiliary loss. AnnealSTE uses SGD, zero initialization, hard k-WTA with a straight-through estimator, and no auxiliary term. Sigmoid uses RMSprop, zero initialization, a soft mask sigma(m), and post-hoc top-k projection. Gumbel-Sigmoid uses Adam, initialization at sigma^{-1}(k/H), the form sigma((m + g_i)/tau_i) with logistic noise, and the budget penalty (sum sigma(m) - k)^2 / k^2.

Replay buffer. The replay buffer stores 200 (x, y, m) triples. On every gradient step we sample one triple whose stored mask has fractional overlap rho = (m_cur . m_j)/||m_cur||_1 in (0.2, 0.8] with the current mask. Entries with rho <= 0.2 are skipped because they are unlikely to share useful computation with the current update; entries with rho > 0.8 are skipped because the current minibatch already trains a very similar mask.

Reconfig protocol. At evaluation time we draw a labeled support set from the target task and run the configurer on it to obtain a recall mask. The model is not told which task generated the support set. Metrics are computed on a larger held-out batch from the same task, disjoint from the support set.

SupSup baseline. We instantiate SupSup on the same parallel-MLP bank (H = 8192 slots, per-MLP topology identical to FTN's). The bank's parallel-MLP weights are initialized once and frozen for the rest of training; dropout on the bank output is disabled for SupSup so the frozen features are deterministic. For each task t we instantiate (i) a fresh score vector s_t in R^H and (ii) a fresh per-task readout head W_t initialized by Xavier-uniform. The forward pass uses a hard top-k mask via the straight-through estimator mask = topk(s_t, k) + s_t - detach(s_t); both s_t and W_t receive gradient through the task loss, the bank does not. Optimizers: Adam, lr = 1e-2 on s_t and 3e-4 on W_t. Per-task storage is the pair (topk(s_t, k), W_t). At evaluation (no task identity) the support-set retrieval scans every stored pair, swaps in the candidate readout, computes the support cross-entropy, and returns the lowest-loss pair; the held-out batch is then evaluated under that pair. This is the deterministic, one-hot version of SupSup's retrieval.

Progressive Networks baseline. We instantiate Progressive Networks on the same parallel-MLP bank. Task t owns a disjoint k-block "column" covering slots [t*k, (t+1)*k). During training of task t: (i) the parallel-MLP weights for slots outside that range are frozen (gradients zeroed before the optimizer step), so all prior columns are untouched; (ii) the active mask is the cumulative mask m_t = indicator[slot < (t+1)*k], so the per-task readout head W_t takes input from the new column AND all previously trained frozen columns (the lateral channel); (iii) only W_t and the new-column slot weights receive gradient. Per-task storage is the pair (m_t, W_t) where m_t is the cumulative mask. At evaluation the same support-set retrieval as SupSup is used. Hyperparameters match FTN (k = 512, Adam lr = 3e-4, no replay). For task counts up to floor(H/k) = 16 no slot wraps; the four benchmarks here use 6, 10, 10, and 12 tasks respectively, so column allocation never collides. This adaptation preserves the two defining properties of Progressive Networks (frozen prior capacity and lateral access for forward transfer); the only deviations are that columns are sub-blocks of a single backbone (rather than separately allocated networks) and that we use support-set retrieval at evaluation rather than task identity.


APPENDIX B. FTN-LoRA IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS

Backbone and bank. We use Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct loaded in bfloat16 as the frozen backbone (28 transformer layers, hidden 1536, intermediate 8960). The LoRA bank wraps every {q, k, v, o} projection inside attention and every {up, gate, down} projection inside the MLP, for a total of 196 banked linear layers. Each banked layer carries H = 128 rank-r = 4 atoms with A_j in R^{in x r} and B_j in R^{r x out} (standard LoRA initialization: A Kaiming-uniform, B zero). The forward through a banked linear is

    y = W_base x + (alpha / k) * sum_{j=1..H} m_j (B_j A_j^T x),

with alpha = 16 and dropout p = 0.05 applied to the bank input. The implementation contracts both the per-atom and rank dimensions in a single einsum (...kr,kro->...o) so the per-atom output dimension is never materialized; without this contraction the support-set inner loop's activation memory blows up at the large MLP intermediate dimension. Total bank parameter count is about 591M (about 39% of the backbone parameters).

Mask configurer. The same AnnealSTE configurer is used at the LoRA bank's slot dimension. Inner-loop count S = 3 (vs S = 30 for the MLP suite); at the LLM scale each inner-loop forward through the model is much more expensive, and three iterations were sufficient on the support batch. Logit learning rate is 0.5 with SGD, zero initialization. During the inner loop we set requires_grad = False on the bank and disable gradient checkpointing on the backbone (we need only the gradient with respect to the mask logits). This keeps the configurer's peak memory comparable to one ordinary forward.

Continual-learning loop. Each task is trained for 100 gradient steps at batch size 4 (no gradient accumulation), sequence length 384, AdamW with lr = 2e-4 and no weight decay on the bank. For FTN-LoRA, every training step runs the configurer on a fresh 4-example support sub-batch from the current task to obtain the active mask, then takes a single gradient step on the query batch through the active atoms. The mask-overlap-filtered replay buffer holds 200 (x, y, m) triples; on every 10th step a random current-task example is added with its current mask, and on every step a replay sample is drawn whose stored mask has fractional overlap rho in (0.1, 0.7] with the current mask. Gradients on the query and replay batches are summed before the AdamW step. SeqLoRA + ER uses a uniform-random buffer of the same capacity and a single replay sample per step.

Task suite and prompt format. Six tasks are presented in canonical order: C-STANCE-A (Chinese stance detection, three-class: Support / Against / Neutral), Financial PhraseBank (English finance sentiment, three-class), TREC (English question type, six-class), dair-ai/emotion (English emotion, six-class), AG News (English topic, four-class), DBpedia-14 (English ontology, fourteen-class). The suite is selected to span heterogeneity along language (Chinese and English), label-space size (2 to 14 classes), and domain. It is an assembled continual-classification benchmark, not a replication of any prior named benchmark. Per task we use 400 training examples, 100 evaluation examples, and a fixed 16-example labeled support set; train and eval are stratified across labels for class-balanced sampling. Every example is wrapped in the Qwen2.5 chat template with a fixed system message and the loss is masked to the answer tokens (the prompt receives no gradient signal).

Metrics. We report final accuracy (ACC), forgetting measure (FM), and backward transfer (BWT) on the lower-triangular performance matrix R[i,j]. Accuracy is the exact-match accuracy on the first answer token; head-start NLL is the answer-token negative log likelihood on a held-out batch, computed with the configurer's selected mask (or the method's eval-time mask for retrieval baselines) before any gradient update on the new task. We use 64 held-out examples for head-start. Five seeds per method.

Compute footprint. Each method completes the six-task sequence in 4-8 minutes (non-FTN methods) or 25-30 minutes (FTN-LoRA family, which adds S = 3 inner-loop forward/backward passes through the model per training step) on a single 80 GiB H100.

Datasets, libraries, and licenses. The MNIST suite is used under its standard academic terms (CC BY-SA 3.0). The LoRA-bank experiment uses six public Hugging Face datasets loaded via the datasets library: C-STANCE-A (MIT), Financial PhraseBank (CC BY-NC-SA 3.0), TREC (no explicit license; standard academic use), dair-ai/emotion (CC BY 4.0; research-only redistribution), AG News (custom, academic non-commercial), and DBpedia-14 (CC BY-SA 3.0). The Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct backbone is used as released under the Qwen Research License with training restricted to the LoRA atoms (the backbone is frozen). Code is built on PyTorch (BSD-3-Clause) and Hugging Face Transformers (Apache 2.0).


APPENDIX C. FTN-LoRA: B_0 INITIALIZATION, ABLATIONS, AND THE ROUTING-VS-RANDOM CONTROL

This appendix documents two design choices for the LoRA-bank experiment: (i) the non-standard initialization B_0 ~ Normal(0, 0.01) in place of the usual B_0 = 0, and (ii) the random-per-batch-mask ablation that isolates the contribution of the support-conditioned configurer from the rest of the FTN-LoRA training pipeline.

Standard B_0 = 0 initialization. Standard LoRA initializes A from a Kaiming-uniform distribution and B to all zeros, so the adapter contributes nothing on step 0 and the fine-tuned model starts at the base backbone exactly. We first ran FTN-LoRA with this initialization. Retention was already reasonable (FTN-LoRA B_0 = 0 + Router reaches ACC 0.76 +/- 0.04, FM 0.19 +/- 0.02 across two seeds), but the configurer's head-start NLL was substantially worse than the retrieval baselines: 1.66 +/- 0.17 (FTN B_0 = 0) and 1.95 +/- 0.35 (FTN B_0 = 0 + Router) versus 1.22-1.32 for the matched-backbone baselines. The configurer was producing masks that actively hurt the new task.

Diagnosis. With B_j exactly zero, atom j's contribution to the bank output is x A_j B_j = 0 irrespective of A_j, so the partial derivative of the loss with respect to m_j is 0. Under the AnnealSTE straight-through estimator, the gradient on the mask logit for atom j is therefore also zero: the inner loop receives no signal about whether atom j would help on the new task. It can deselect atoms whose previous-task training is actively misaligned with the support set, but it cannot pull useful untrained atoms into the active set without first selecting them by chance. The parallel-MLP backbone avoids this because every parallel MLP, trained or not, contributes a stochastic non-zero output, so the configurer's gradient on the mask is informative about every atom from step 0.

B_0 ~ Normal(0, 0.01). We replace zero initialization with a small Gaussian, B_0 ~ Normal(0, 0.01). The model still starts close to the base backbone (the per-atom contribution is small), but every atom produces a non-zero output that gives the configurer a non-zero gradient signal on its mask logit. This change unlocks the FTN-LoRA family results: ACC rises from 0.62 to 0.83, FM falls from 0.28 to 0.09, and BWT flips from -0.20 to +0.19, all within multi-seed sample noise of the random-per-batch-mask ablation that shares the same training pipeline.

The random-per-batch-mask ablation. A reasonable concern is that the small non-zero B_0 injects useful adapter perturbations on its own, and that the headline FTN-LoRA gains therefore reflect a non-FTN cause. We test this with a five-seed control: FTN-LoRA random per-batch masks keeps the FTN-LoRA training pipeline (mask-overlap-filtered replay, B_0 ~ Normal(0, 0.01), the same 100-step schedule and active-parameter budget, the same per-batch reconfiguration cadence) but replaces the support-conditioned configurer with a fresh random k-of-H mask each step. The eval-time configurer is unchanged. The result decomposes the headline cleanly: the random-mask ablation matches FTN-LoRA on retention (ACC 0.85 +/- 0.12 vs 0.83 +/- 0.10, FM 0.12 +/- 0.07 vs 0.09 +/- 0.09) and on head-start NLL (1.38 +/- 0.18 vs 1.42 +/- 0.25), so the training pipeline rather than the configurer is the source of those numbers; but it does not flip backward transfer (-0.08 +/- 0.12 vs +0.19 +/- 0.10), so the support-conditioned configurer specifically is what produces FTN-LoRA's positive BWT.

What this jointly establishes and what it does not. The two ablations together support a narrower mechanism claim than the controlled MLP suite's: at LoRA scale the support-conditioned configurer is the load-bearing piece for backward transfer specifically, while the headline retention is explained by the training pipeline (filtered replay, non-zero B_0, per-batch reconfiguration on a wide bank). The LoRA experiment is silent on head-start: every method lands in the narrow band 1.22-1.42. The parallel-MLP suite avoids this confound by construction (correlated synthetic and shared-shuffle MNIST have hand-tuned concept overlap), and remains the appropriate probe of FTN's head-start claim. BWT separates on the LoRA suite because it depends on training-time bank dynamics across tasks rather than on any specific task pair's cross-task structure. What we have not done at LoRA scale: a B_0 scale sweep, a Qwen-7B/14B scale-up, an order-sensitivity check, or an LLM stream with measurable cross-task structure that would let head-start adjudicate the configurer's pre-update mechanism.


APPENDIX D. SECONDARY EXPERIMENTS AND SWEEPS

Primary configurer sweep. We sweep configurer type in {Sigmoid, AnnealSTE, Gumbel-Sigmoid} and inner-step budget S in {10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60} on the correlated synthetic family with k = 512, support size 256, and per-batch reconfiguration. The sweep uses one shared seed to compare the 18 configurations within seed; subsequent ablations report mean and standard deviation over 8 seeds. All three configurers reach low forgetting (FM in [0.04, 0.06]) and similar accuracy (ACC in [0.89, 0.92]). The discriminating axis is head-start: AnnealSTE attains the best head-start loss at every budget (mean 0.37 across S, vs 0.46 for Sigmoid and 0.55 for Gumbel-Sigmoid) and is stable across budgets, while Gumbel-Sigmoid improves steadily with S but does not reach AnnealSTE's level. AnnealSTE at S = 30 gives head-start 0.358, FM = 0.049, r_share = 0.509; doubling the budget to S = 60 yields no head-start gain (0.386) at twice the inner-loop cost. We therefore adopt AnnealSTE, S = 30, k = 512, support size 256, and per-batch reconfiguration as the default.

Baseline endpoints. Comparing FTN with two endpoints on the same parallel bank: NoMask trains the full bank on every task (full sharing); FixedMask assigns deterministic disjoint blocks of k neurons (oracle isolation with no mask inference or sharing). FTN lies between these endpoints. On synthetic tasks, NoMask has FM = 0.392 +/- 0.016, FixedMask has 0 by construction, and FTN obtains 0.055 +/- 0.015, closing about 86% of the forgetting gap. On shared-shuffle MNIST, FTN closes about 97% of the gap. Unlike FixedMask, FTN also improves head-start loss on correlated tasks and recovers nonzero concept-sharing structure.

Reconfiguration frequency. Per-batch reconfiguration is central to FTN's transfer behavior. A natural alternative is to configure once at the beginning of a task block and reuse that mask for the entire task. This per-task variant creates cleaner stored masks but trains a narrower set of subnetworks. In contrast, per-batch reconfiguration exposes many nearby masks to gradient signal, creating a broader pool of partially trained assemblies that future support-set optimizations can recombine. Empirically, per-batch reconfiguration gives lower head-start loss and lower forgetting on the correlated synthetic family. Per-batch obtains head-start loss 0.425 +/- 0.081 and FM = 0.055 +/- 0.014, while per-task gives 0.548 +/- 0.103 and 0.100 +/- 0.025. Per-task has higher r_share (0.611 +/- 0.105 versus 0.429 +/- 0.195), consistent with a more stable stored mask, but the per-batch regime is better for forward transfer under support-set reconfiguration.

Sparsity and support-set size. The top-k budget controls how much overlap is possible. Sweeping k in {512, 128, 32, 8} shows a clear isolation-transfer trade-off. Reducing k lowers FM from 0.056 at k = 512 to 0.032 at k = 32, then 0.039 at k = 8, while head-start loss rises from about 0.42 to 0.60. Sharing fidelity improves from 0.455 to 0.624 as k decreases to 32, then falls slightly at k = 8. Moderate sparsity gives the cleanest structured overlap; extreme sparsity improves isolation but leaves less reusable basis for future tasks. The support size used by the configurer affects transfer more than retention. When support size decreases from 256 to 4, head-start loss rises from 0.425 +/- 0.080 to 1.339 +/- 0.095, reflecting poorer generalization from the support set to held-out examples. In contrast, FM remains at or below 0.060 across the sweep, and ACC stays in the 0.86-0.89 range. Thus the continual-learning protection is robust to small supports, while forward transfer depends on enough examples to infer a useful mask.

Mask overlap statistics. The pairwise mask-overlap fraction S[i,j]/K, averaged over off-diagonal entries and at its largest off-diagonal pair, measures neuron reuse. S[i,j] is the count of neurons active in both task i's and task j's stored mask; K is the per-task budget. The chance baseline for uniformly random K-of-H subsets is K/H with H = 8192. Across the AnnealSTE configurations, overlap is well above chance, confirming that the configurer reuses neurons rather than reducing to fixed disjoint subnetworks. The NoMask and FixedMask baselines bracket the range at 1.0 and 0.0.
